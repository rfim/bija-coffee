# Coffee stories narration: recording script

Every paragraph of Coffee stories (`stories.html`) plays its own clip from this folder. The current voice is a synthetic British voice (Kokoro, bf_emma), so the page works today.

**To replace it with Bu Devi's voice:** record each paragraph below as its own clip, save it as MP3 with exactly the file name shown, and overwrite the file here. Nothing in the code changes; the page picks up the new clips on the next deploy. Clips can be swapped one chapter at a time, though one voice per chapter sounds best.

Recording tips: a quiet room, phone about a hand's width from the mouth, a second of silence at each end, calm reading pace. MP3, mono, 44.1 kHz, 96–128 kbps.

The Brand identity page also plays `audio/bija-say.mp3`: one word, "Bija" (/ˈbɪdʒa/).


## java

**java-01.mp3**

Long before it reached Java, coffee had already travelled a remarkable distance. Arabica is native to Ethiopia; across the Red Sea, Yemen became a hub of cultivation and commerce, and by the sixteenth century the drink was circulating through trading and cultural networks far removed from the slopes where it grew.

**java-02.mp3**

The Yemeni harbour of Mocha (also spelled Mokha or Mokka) became so bound up with that trade that its name outlived its geography. Centuries before it evoked chocolate, “mocha” simply meant a port where coffee changed hands.

**java-03.mp3**

Early in the seventeenth century the Dutch trader Pieter van den Broecke encountered coffee there, at a moment when European companies were striving to break into a long-established Indian Ocean trade. Tales of smuggled seedlings make a tempting first chapter, yet the documented path to Java unfolded over decades, in distinct and separate stages.

**java-04.mp3**

Seedlings arrived in Java from Malabar, in India, in 1696. Floods destroyed that first planting; a second introduction in 1699 took hold. Cultivation spread outwards from the country around Batavia, today’s Jakarta, into West Java, and by the early eighteenth century the island was shipping coffee to Europe, with Indonesia’s first exports usually dated to 1711. So close did the association become that “java” passed into English as a byword for coffee itself.

**java-05.mp3**

Around 1706 living plants were sent from Java to the Hortus botanical garden in Amsterdam, and from there cuttings helped carry cultivation to other corners of the world. Java was not where the story began, but it profoundly altered where coffee could grow and how far it could travel.

**java-06.mp3**

Botanical curiosity was only half of it: these routes were also forged by colonial power. Coffee enriched the Dutch East India Company while local communities shouldered forced cultivation and tightly controlled trade. An honest account of coffee makes room for that, and turns our attention to the farmers, cooperatives and producers whose expertise shapes Indonesian coffee today.

**java-07.mp3**

No single flavour can speak for Indonesian coffee. At Bija, Sumatra offers the depth of wet-hulled lots alongside fruit-laden naturals; Java brings the earthy richness of Old Brown Java and the layered character of our House Blend; Bali Kintamani strikes a softer balance of cacao and citrus. Start with whichever flavour intrigues you, and trace it back to its place.


## roots

**roots-01.mp3**

Volcanic slopes, rainforest and highland farming communities: each of Indonesia’s coffee regions has its own history and growing conditions. By the USDA’s 2025/26 figures, Indonesia is the world’s fourth-largest coffee producer, and a global count in November 2025, reported by IndonesiaBaik, ranked it first for the sheer number of coffee shops, from modern cafés to the humble warung kopi.

**roots-02.mp3**

Once linked to the Sanskrit name Suvarnadvipa, the “Island of Gold”, Sumatra is spined by the Bukit Barisan range, where rainforest climbs into cool highlands; Kerinci Seblat, Gunung Leuser and Bukit Barisan Selatan shelter vital forest habitat. Coffee spread here in the nineteenth century, partly through forced cultivation under Dutch rule in West Sumatra. Today Aceh, Lake Toba and Kerinci each bring their own terrain, communities and processing traditions.

**roots-03.mp3**

Indonesia’s most populous island, home to more than half the nation at the 2020 census. Beyond its cities lie farmed plains, volcanic slopes and mountain forest, with coffee thriving in the cooler uplands. After a first planting in Batavia was lost to floods, a second introduction in 1699 spread through the Priangan highlands; exports began in 1711, and “java” has meant coffee ever since.

**roots-04.mp3**

In the Kintamani highlands, coffee grows more than 900 metres up the volcanic flanks of Mount Batur, in cool air and fertile soil beneath shade trees. The tradition dates to the early nineteenth century; farmers organise through subak abian, customary groups that bind agriculture to community and spiritual life, and in 2008 Kintamani Arabica became Indonesia’s first registered geographical indication.


## house

**house-01.mp3**

Our House Blend marries naturally processed Arabica from Cisurupan in West Java with specialty-grade Robusta from the Petungkriyono Forest in Central Java. Rather than letting one coffee dominate, we ask each to contribute what it does best, then hold the two in equilibrium.

**house-02.mp3**

The Arabica supplies the fruit: chocolate, grapefruit and sweet raisin. The Robusta lends a lower, darker register of pine, spice and clove. Together they produce a cup with warmth, texture and a finish that lingers.

**house-03.mp3**

A single origin is a close-up of one place; a blend is a conversation between two. Here highland Arabica meets lower-lying Robusta, fruit meets spice, sweetness meets earth. Both are naturally processed, dried inside the cherry, which gives them common ground while each keeps its own accent. A dark roast then draws the layers into a full-bodied, everyday coffee.

**house-04.mp3**

The Arabica grows in the shaded home gardens of West Java, woven into a living landscape rather than cleared from it. Through the Little Fireface Project, farmers and conservationists work side by side to protect the endangered Javan slow loris and its habitat, and the coffee is Wildlife Friendly® certified.

**house-05.mp3**

The Robusta comes from Central Java’s Petungkriyono Forest, where SwaraOwa works with the communities living around the habitat of the Javan gibbon. Its coffee programme pairs conservation with training, helping growers produce better coffee. This Robusta, too, is Wildlife Friendly® certified, a reminder that thoughtful sourcing starts with the people and landscapes behind the beans.

**house-06.mp3**

Kindred Forest, our partner in sourcing and roasting Indonesian specialty coffee, made the pairing possible. Expect chocolate, grapefruit and raisin, giving way to pine, spice, clove and an earthy, herbal close. Drink it black to explore its layers, or with milk for a mellower, rounder cup.
